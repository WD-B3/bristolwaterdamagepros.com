Water extraction is the removal of standing water from a structure using pumps and truck-mounted or portable extraction units. It is the first stage of any flood or leak job, and it comes before drying, because dehumidifiers cannot work against an inch of water sitting on the slab.

This page is about water that is on the floor right now. If the water came from a specific source, such as a burst pipe or a sewer backup, that page covers the shut-off steps. If the floor is only damp, structural drying is the better fit.

When standing water is an emergency

Treat any standing water as urgent if it is deeper than a damp film, if it is spreading toward finished rooms or if it touches electrical outlets, baseboard heaters or an electrical panel. Water also becomes more contaminated the longer it sits, and carpet pad and drywall begin to wick it upward within hours.

While you wait for a crew, shut off the main water valve if the water is still running, lift valuables and small furniture off the floor and turn off power to wet areas only if the panel is dry and reachable. If water reaches an outlet or panel, stay out and call the utility or 911 before calling us.

What an extraction crew actually does

A crew starts by finding the water's edge and depth, then sets pumps in the deepest low point. Portable extraction units follow, pulling water out of carpet and pad and off hard floors, and a moisture meter confirms how far the water traveled into walls and subfloors.

  • Submersible pumps for depths of several inches.
  • Portable and truck-mounted extraction units for carpet, pad and hard floors.
  • Moisture meters and thermal imaging to find water behind walls.
  • Removal of saturated materials that cannot be dried in place.

Extraction does not finish the job. Once the standing water is gone, drying equipment takes over, and the structural drying stage can last several days.

Why Bristol basements and garages take on water

Bristol is a bay town, and heavy nor'easters and tropical storms push rain and surge toward low ground. The National Weather Service records a storm surge of 12 to 15 feet in Narragansett Bay during the 1938 hurricane. Your own risk depends on your address, so look it up on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center, and ask the town building inspection office at 10 Court Street about permit rules for rebuilding.

Many Bristol homes near the harbor also have older foundations of stone or block, which can seep at the joints. A sump pump that fails during a storm can put several inches of water on a basement floor within an hour. Water that comes up through a floor drain may be sewage, and that changes the safety rules, so see flood damage cleanup for rising water.

Common questions about water extraction in Bristol

How soon should standing water be removed?

As soon as it is safe, because water that sits begins soaking into drywall, pad and framing. Call dispatch while you shut off the source, and a coordinator will tell you what to do next.

Can I use a wet vacuum myself?

A household wet vacuum handles a small spill. It will not keep up with several inches of water, and it is not safe to use near outlets or in contaminated water.

What happens after the water is out?

Drying starts immediately. Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings return to normal, which commonly takes several days. Ask the crew for the readings so you can see the progress.

Do you handle water extraction emergencies at night or on weekends in Bristol?

Yes. The dispatch line is answered 24/7, including nights, weekends and holidays, and same-day response is available when a crew is free. We do not promise an arrival time, but we do tell you how to limit the water while you wait.
