How to Check a Home for Water Damage Before Buying in Bristol, RI

Water damage is easier to negotiate before closing than to fix after. Here is what to look for on a walk-through.

By the Bristol Water Damage Pros team

Emergency Dispatch
(401) 598-8555
Call Now

Buying a home near the water means asking harder questions about moisture. Past leaks may have been repaired well, or covered with paint. A careful walk-through, a home inspection and a few direct questions to the seller can tell you which.

This checklist is for buyers in Bristol and the East Bay. It is not a substitute for a licensed home inspector.

Start with ceilings and walls

Look for stains, fresh paint in one patch, bubbling and cracked plaster. A ceiling with a clean patch of new paint under a bathroom or roofline is worth a question. Run your hand along baseboards to feel for soft spots or swelling.

Check the basement and foundation

Look for a chalky line on the wall that marks past water, rust at the base of steel posts, efflorescence (white powder) on masonry and a sump pump with a discharge line. Ask where the sump discharges, since a connection to the sewer can be a problem. Smell the air for mustiness.

Floors and flooring

Walk the floors and note cupping in hardwood, soft laminate and carpet that squishes. A hollow sound or a slope near an exterior door can indicate water under the floor. See hardwood floor damage for what cupping looks like.

Plumbing and appliances

Open the cabinet under every sink and look for stains and soft wood. Check the water heater for rust and corrosion at the base. Look at hoses on the washer and dishwasher. Run water and flush toilets to see if anything leaks below.

  • Stains or fresh patch paint on ceilings.
  • Chalky lines or rust in the basement.
  • Cupped or soft flooring.
  • Corrosion at the water heater base.
  • Musty smell in closets or the cellar.

Ask the right questions

Ask the seller whether there has been any water damage, any insurance claim or any repair for leaks, and ask for records. Ask your agent to request a claims history report. In many states sellers must disclose known defects, and a written answer is better than a verbal one.

If you find active moisture, a restoration company can take readings and tell you what drying involves. See basement water cleanup for what a wet cellar involves.

Inspections worth asking for

A general home inspection looks at the roof, plumbing and basement, but it is not a moisture survey. If you see signs of past water, ask for a moisture meter reading and a thermal camera scan of suspect walls and ceilings. A sewer scope can check the line from the house to the street.

For older homes, ask an inspector who knows plaster, stone foundations and original plumbing. Those materials behave differently from new construction, and a general checklist can miss them.

Negotiating after you find damage

Water damage is not always a reason to walk away. A repaired leak with receipts is different from an unexplained stain. If you find active damage, ask the seller to repair it, offer a credit or adjust the price. Get a written scope from a restoration company so the numbers are real.

Read the disclosure form carefully. A blank or vague answer about water problems deserves a follow-up question.

After you move in

Locate the water main and the sump pump on day one. Test the sump by pouring water into the pit. Check the washer hoses and the water heater, and take photos of the home's condition for your own records. Those few minutes can save a lot of trouble later.

Exterior clues

Walk the outside before you go in. Look at the grading near the foundation, the downspouts and the gutters. Water should flow away from the house. Check for staining on the foundation, cracks in masonry and soil that slopes toward the walls.

Look at the roof from the ground and from the attic. Dark stains on the underside of the roof deck, damp insulation and rusted nails point to past or present leaks.

Attic and crawl space

Attics and crawl spaces are where hidden moisture lives. Bring a flashlight and look for mildew, stained framing and insulation that is compressed or discolored. Ask your inspector to open the access hatches. A short look can say a lot about how the house has been kept.

Documents to request

Ask for the seller's disclosure, any records of insurance claims on the property and receipts for plumbing, roofing and basement work. Ask whether the home has ever had a sump pump installed or a water heater replaced. Records turn a vague story into something you can verify.

Your agent or attorney can help you request documents in writing before the inspection period ends.

Weighing what you find

Not every stain is a deal breaker. A single old ring above a window that was fixed and documented is a small matter. Active moisture in a foundation wall, repeated stains in several rooms or a musty smell throughout the house deserve more attention.

Use the findings to ask for repairs, a credit or more time. A written scope from a restoration company puts a number on the work and keeps the conversation factual.

Bristol flood and records tools

Look up the property on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. Bristol is on Narragansett Bay, and the National Weather Service records that the 1938 hurricane's surge reached 12 to 15 feet in the bay.

The Rhode Island Division of Insurance states that a standard homeowners policy does not cover flood, and that flood policies usually have a 30-day waiting period. Ask about flood coverage before you close.
