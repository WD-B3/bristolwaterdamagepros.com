How Long Does Mold Take to Grow After Water Damage in Bristol, RI?

The window is short, which is why drying starts before cleanup.

By the Bristol Water Damage Pros team

Emergency Dispatch
(401) 598-8555
Call Now

People ask how fast mold appears because they want to know how long they have. The common guidance in the restoration field is that mold can begin to grow within about 24 to 48 hours on materials that stay wet, but visible growth may take longer to show. The exact timeline depends on the material, temperature and humidity.

The practical point is simple. The faster materials are dried, the less chance mold has to start.

What mold needs

Mold needs moisture, a food source and a suitable temperature. Drywall paper, wood, carpet and dust provide food, and indoor temperatures are fine for growth. The one thing you can remove is moisture.

The first two days

During the first day or two, wet porous materials are at risk. This is the window when extraction and drying make the most difference. After that, hidden growth may begin inside walls or under flooring, where you cannot see it.

Where growth hides

Look behind baseboards, under carpet pad, inside wall cavities and on the back of drywall. A musty smell with no visible source is a reason to take moisture readings. A crew with a meter and thermal camera can find wet areas without tearing everything open.

What you can do in the first day

  • Stop the water source.
  • Remove standing water and wet items.
  • Run fans and a dehumidifier.
  • Call a restoration company for extraction and drying.
  • Photograph the damage for your insurer.

If you already see growth, read mold remediation. For drying, see structural drying.

What does not work

Spraying disinfectant on wet drywall, painting over a stain or turning up the heat without dehumidifying does not remove the moisture that feeds growth. A damp material needs to be dried or removed.

Factors that speed or slow growth

Warm temperatures, high humidity, poor airflow and porous materials speed growth. Cool temperatures, low humidity and fast drying slow it. A basement in summer is a classic fast case, and a well-ventilated room in winter a slower one.

Indoor humidity above about 60 percent can support growth on its own, even without a leak. A cheap humidity meter helps you watch your home.

Visible signs

  • Fuzzy or speckled patches in black, green or white.
  • A musty or earthy smell.
  • Peeling or bubbling paint.
  • Discolored grout or caulk.
  • Allergy-like symptoms that ease when you leave the building, a question for a physician.

What to do if more than two days have passed

Do not panic, but do not wait. Dry the area, remove wet materials that cannot be saved and have a crew take moisture readings. If growth is visible, avoid disturbing it and call for an assessment. Acting now keeps a small problem from becoming a large one.

Growth in different materials

Drywall paper is a favorite food for mold, and a wall that stays wet can show growth quickly on the back side. Wood framing is slower. Carpet and pad hold water and organic debris and can grow mold in a couple of days if not dried.

Concrete and block do not feed mold themselves, but dust and finishes on them can. Basements often show growth on stored boxes and wooden shelves first.

Stopping the clock

Drying fast is the best way to prevent growth. Extract standing water, remove wet porous materials and run dehumidifiers. If you cannot start within a day, call a professional. A crew with the right equipment can bring humidity down and measure moisture in places you cannot reach.

A quick self-check after a leak

  • Was the water cleaned up within a day.
  • Are carpet, pad and drywall dried or removed.
  • Does the area smell musty.
  • Do you see staining or spots.
  • Has a moisture reading confirmed it is dry.

If you cannot answer yes to the first, second and last questions, have the area checked.

What changes in cold weather

In winter, cold surfaces cause condensation, and humid indoor air can drop moisture on windows, exterior walls and ceilings. Mold can grow in those spots even without a leak. Ventilate bathrooms and kitchens, and keep furniture a few inches from cold walls.

If a wet wall never seems to dry, a moisture reading can tell you whether it is condensation or a leak.

Why prompt help matters

Once growth starts, the job changes from drying to remediation, which involves containment and removal. That is a larger and more expensive scope than drying alone. Calling within the first day keeps the job in the smaller category in many cases.

Even if more time has passed, a crew can still help. Take readings, remove what is wet and plan the next step.

Professional mold work follows ANSI/IICRC S520, the published standard for mold remediation, and water damage work follows ANSI/IICRC S500.

Bristol's wet-weather pattern

The National Weather Service recorded 6 to 10 inches of rain in March 2010, and every Rhode Island county received a federal disaster declaration. After long wet stretches like that, groundwater rises and basements stay damp for days.

A Rhode Island environmental document on the Town of Bristol's wastewater system describes wet-weather sewer overflows and names sump pumps tied into the sewer as a major cause. A basement that backs up in a storm may need sanitizing as well as drying.

More questions about this topic

Does a wet basement after heavy rain risk mold in Bristol?

Yes, if materials stay damp. Dry or remove wet materials quickly and run a dehumidifier.
