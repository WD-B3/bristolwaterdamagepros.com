How to Get Rid of Black Mold in a Bristol, RI Home

Size, material and moisture decide whether black mold is a DIY job or a professional one.

By the Bristol Water Damage Pros team

Emergency Dispatch
(401) 598-8555
Call Now

Black mold is the common name for dark mold growth, and it appears in damp corners, around windows and behind leaking fixtures. Color does not tell you what kind of mold it is, and a lab test is the only way to identify a species. What matters in practice is how much there is, what it is growing on and whether the moisture source has been fixed.

Health questions belong with a physician. This article covers the cleaning side only.

Find and stop the moisture first

Mold grows where materials stay damp. Cleaning a patch without fixing the leak, the condensation or the ventilation problem means the growth is likely to return. Check for plumbing leaks, roof leaks, window condensation and a bathroom fan that does not vent outside.

When a small patch can be cleaned

A small patch on a hard, non-porous surface such as tile, glass or sealed metal can be cleaned with detergent and water and dried fully. Wear gloves, eye protection and a well-fitting respirator. Do not mix cleaning products, since some combinations release dangerous fumes. Keep the work area closed off.

When it needs a professional

Call a remediation company when growth covers a large area or sits on drywall, ceiling tile or insulation. Call when it follows a flood or sewage loss, or when you smell mold but cannot see it. The ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, published for professional mold remediation, describes containment and removal practices that a household cleaning does not match.

  • Growth on porous materials such as drywall and carpet.
  • Patches larger than a small area.
  • Growth that returns after cleaning.
  • Mold after sewage or floodwater.
  • Musty smell with no visible source.

What not to do

Do not dry-brush or sand mold, since that spreads spores. Do not spray bleach on porous materials and call it done, since it does not remove the growth inside drywall or wood. Do not paint over it. Painting over mold hides it and traps moisture.

Testing and clearance

Sampling can show whether spores are elevated compared with outdoor air, but it is not a pass or fail on its own. If you want testing, ask who interprets the results. Many scopes are based on visible growth and moisture readings alone.

Protective gear and basic precautions

A well-fitting respirator rated for particles, sealed goggles, gloves and clothes you can wash immediately are the minimum for any cleaning. Seal the room with plastic sheeting and tape, turn off the forced-air system to the room and avoid fans that blow air across the growth.

Bag debris in thick plastic before carrying it through the house. Wipe surfaces with a damp cloth rather than dry dusting, and wash your clothes separately after the job.

What cleaning cannot fix

Cleaning removes growth from a surface, but it does not repair a leak, a bad vent or a damp foundation. If the cause remains, growth is likely to return in the same spot. That is why a good assessment looks for the moisture source before it talks about cleanup.

If you plan to sell or rent the home, keep photos of the area before and after cleaning and any invoices for professional work. Those records show that the problem was found and handled.

Costs of waiting

Small patches grow, and a patch you ignore this month may involve a wall next season. Wet drywall that stays in place can need to be cut out, and the repair stage adds to the bill. Early action almost always costs less than late action.

A realistic cleaning plan for a small hard-surface patch

Set up before you start. Close the door, cover vents, gather bags, detergent, rags and a bucket. Dampen the surface lightly so spores do not rise, scrub gently, wipe with clean water and dry the area fully. Throw away rags and sponges in a sealed bag when you finish.

Check the spot again over the next two weeks. If growth returns, the moisture problem has not been solved, and the next step is a moisture check, not a stronger cleaner.

Choosing a remediation company

Ask whether the company follows the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, how it contains the work area and what it does with removed materials. Ask for a written scope that names the rooms, the materials to be removed and how the area will be dried. Be careful of any quote that promises results that cannot be verified.

After the cleanup

Keep the area dry and watch it for a few weeks. Check humidity with a meter and look at the spot each week. If growth returns, the moisture source is still active, and the next step is a closer look at plumbing, roofing or ventilation.

Protecting the rest of the home

While you work, keep doors closed, seal vents and avoid carrying debris through clean rooms. After cleaning, wipe nearby surfaces with a damp cloth and vacuum with a HEPA filter if you have one.

Wash your clothes right away and shower. Careful habits keep a small project from becoming a house-wide one.

Why Bristol bathrooms and basements grow mold

Bristol sits on a humid bay, and the National Weather Service recorded 6 to 10 inches of rain across Rhode Island in March 2010. After a long wet period, basements and walls may stay damp for weeks.

A state environmental document on the Town of Bristol's wastewater system names sump pumps tied into the sewer as a major cause of wet-weather overflows, so a basement that backs up should be treated as a contamination problem first and a mold problem second.

More questions about this topic

Can black mold be removed with vinegar or bleach?

They may clean the surface of a hard, non-porous material but do not remove growth from drywall or wood. Large areas need professional containment.
