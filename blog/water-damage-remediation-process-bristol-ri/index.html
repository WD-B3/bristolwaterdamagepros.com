The Water Damage Remediation Process in Bristol, RI

Remediation is a sequence of stages, and each one depends on the one before it. This is what happens, in order.

By the Bristol Water Damage Pros team

Water damage remediation is the process of stopping water damage and returning a building to a dry, safe condition. People often picture a crew showing up with a few fans, but a proper job has stages, measurements and a finish line defined by moisture readings, not by how the room looks.

Knowing the stages helps you judge whether the company you call is doing the real work, and it helps you understand why a job may take several days.

Stage 1: assessment and safety

The crew checks for electrical hazards, structural concerns and the category of the water under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard. They find the source and confirm it has been stopped. A moisture meter and thermal camera map where the water has gone, which is often farther than it appears.

Stage 2: water removal

Standing water is pumped or extracted first, because every gallon removed by machine is one less to evaporate. Extraction units pull water from carpet and hard surfaces. See water extraction for equipment and limits.

Stage 3: removal of unsalvageable materials

Materials that cannot be dried, such as saturated insulation, carpet pad and drywall exposed to contaminated water, are removed. This sometimes surprises homeowners, but leaving them in place holds moisture and feeds mold. A crew should explain what is coming out and why.

Stage 4: drying and dehumidification

Air movers circulate air across wet surfaces and dehumidifiers remove the moisture from the air. Equipment placement is based on readings and is adjusted over the following days. This stage is described in structural drying.

Stage 5: monitoring

A crew returns to read moisture levels in the same spots each visit and records them. Equipment is moved or removed as areas dry. The job ends when materials reach normal readings, not on a fixed day count.

  • Daily or regular moisture readings in marked locations.
  • Adjustment of air movers and dehumidifiers.
  • A log you can give your insurer.
  • Final readings that show materials at normal levels.

Stage 6: cleaning and restoration

Surfaces are cleaned and, where appropriate, treated with an antimicrobial. Repairs follow: drywall, paint, flooring and trim. Mitigation and repair are often separate scopes, and sometimes separate contractors.

What can slow the process

Wet plaster, hardwood and concrete dry slowly. Hidden moisture inside walls or under floors may be found only after the first round of readings. Humid weather makes the job longer, which matters on a bay-side peninsula in summer.

What you can do during the job

Stay out of containment areas, keep pets away from equipment and do not turn machines off to save noise. Equipment is placed for a reason, and moving it can leave a wet pocket. If the noise is a problem, ask the crew whether a machine can be repositioned or whether quieter models are available.

Keep a short log of anything you notice, such as a new smell, a leak that returns or a machine that stops. Share it at the next visit. Small observations often help the crew find hidden water.

Questions to ask any company

  • Which standard do your crews follow for water damage.
  • How will you measure and record moisture each visit.
  • What materials do you expect to remove, and why.
  • What is excluded from the scope you gave me.
  • Who will talk to my insurer if asked.

A company that answers these plainly is usually doing the work properly. Vague answers, or an unwillingness to put the scope in writing, are reasons to keep looking.

After drying is complete

Once readings are normal, the equipment is removed and the site is cleaned. The next stage is repair, which may be done by the same company or by another contractor. Ask for a copy of the final readings so you have a record that the structure was dry when the equipment left.

Mitigation and reconstruction are different contracts

Many homeowners sign one agreement and expect everything to be handled. In practice, the mitigation contract covers drying and the removal of wet materials, while reconstruction covers rebuilding. They may be written separately, with different scopes and different prices.

Ask whether the company you hire handles both. If not, ask for the final readings and photos so another contractor can pick up where the first one left off.

Red flags during a job

Be cautious if a crew never takes readings, removes equipment after a fixed number of days without checking or refuses to explain why a material is coming out. Another warning sign is a company that wants a large payment before any work begins and offers no written scope.

A professional crew welcomes questions. If you cannot get a clear answer about the plan, ask for it in writing.

If you are unsure about any stage, call and ask. A short conversation before a crew starts is easier than a correction afterward.

Bristol conditions that shape drying

Bristol lies on Narragansett Bay, and the National Weather Service recorded 6 to 10 inches of rain in March 2010, after which every county in Rhode Island received a federal disaster declaration. Rain on that scale raises groundwater and saturates basements, which lengthens drying times.

Older Bristol homes within the Waterfront Historic District, which was listed in 1975, often have plaster walls and stone foundations. Both hold moisture longer than modern drywall and poured concrete.

More questions about this topic

Why does drying take so long in an old Bristol home?

Plaster, wood lath and stone foundations release moisture slowly, so readings may take longer to reach normal than in newer construction.
