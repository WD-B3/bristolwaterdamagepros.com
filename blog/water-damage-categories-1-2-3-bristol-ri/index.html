Water Damage Categories 1, 2 and 3 Explained for Bristol, RI Homes

The category of the water decides what can be dried and what must be thrown away.

By the Bristol Water Damage Pros team

Emergency Dispatch
(401) 598-8555
Call Now

Restoration crews sort water into three categories under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration. The category depends on the source of the water and how long it has been there, and it tells the crew how to protect people and what to do with wet materials.

Understanding it helps you follow what a crew tells you and helps you document the loss.

Category 1: clean water

Category 1 comes from a sanitary source such as a supply line, a faucet or a rain event that has not touched anything dirty. Materials can usually be dried in place if the crew acts quickly. Time matters, since clean water that sits can degrade into a worse category.

Category 2: gray water

Category 2 water contains some contamination and may cause illness if consumed. Common sources are washing machine and dishwasher discharge and a toilet overflow without solids. Porous materials such as carpet pad are usually removed, and hard surfaces are cleaned and dried.

Category 3: black water

Category 3 water is grossly contaminated. Sewage, river and harbor flooding and long-standing water belong here. Porous materials are removed and the area is cleaned and sanitized. Stay out of the area and follow sewage backup safety rules.

Water can change category

Clean water that sits for a day or two, flows across dirty floors or touches contaminated materials can be reclassified. This is another reason to start extraction quickly. A crew should tell you what category they assign and why.

  • Do not wade through water you cannot identify.
  • Keep children and pets away.
  • Do not use a household vacuum on wet areas.
  • Photograph the water and its source before cleanup.

Why this matters for your insurance and your cleanup

The category affects what is removed, which affects the scope and the cost. It can also affect coverage, since flood and sewer backups are treated differently from a burst pipe. Ask your insurer how your policy handles each. For flood cleanup, see flood damage cleanup.

How a crew decides the category

The crew looks at the source, how far the water traveled, how long it sat and what it touched. A clean supply line that soaked a carpet for hours is not the same as one that sat for three days. Samples are rarely needed. Judgment and experience guide the call.

If you are unsure, say so. Treat the water as contaminated until a trained person has looked at it.

Protective steps by category

Category 1 jobs need ordinary care, with gloves and good ventilation. Category 2 calls for gloves, boots and eye protection, and Category 3 requires full protective gear, containment and careful disposal. Crews are trained to use the right equipment for each.

Why you should not do Category 3 yourself

Sewage and floodwater can carry bacteria and other contaminants. Cleaning it without training or protection risks illness and may leave contamination behind. Leave Category 3 cleanup to professionals, and keep children, pets and vulnerable family members out of the area.

Common examples by room

In a kitchen, a supply line leak is usually Category 1, a dishwasher overflow is often Category 2 and a sewer backup is Category 3. In a bathroom, a faucet leak is Category 1, a sink overflow with soap and hair is Category 2 and a toilet backup with solids is Category 3.

In a basement, a pipe break is Category 1, but groundwater and floodwater are treated as Category 2 or 3 because they cross soil and surfaces.

What to tell dispatch

Describe the source, how long the water has been there, what it touched and whether anything smells. Mention if any person has contacted it. That information helps the crew prepare the right equipment and gear before they arrive.

Why contamination levels change handling

The higher the category, the more material is removed rather than dried. This is not about being cautious for its own sake. Porous materials soak up contaminants that cleaning cannot reach, so replacing them is the safe option. Hard surfaces can be cleaned and disinfected.

Documenting water category for a claim

Photograph the water, its source and the materials it touched. Note how long it sat. Keep any lab or crew notes that classify the water. These records help your insurer understand the loss.

Ask your restoration company to write the category on its scope. That gives you and your insurer a shared starting point.

What to do while you wait for help

Keep people and pets out of the water. Turn off power only if the panel is dry and reachable. Open windows if weather allows and keep children away. Do not try to clean up Category 3 water yourself.

Tell dispatch what you see, and follow the coordinator's instructions until a crew arrives.

Category 3 risks in a bay town

Bristol sits on Narragansett Bay, and the National Weather Service records that Hurricane Carol in 1954 brought a surge of 10 to 15 feet across Rhode Island. Surge and harbor floodwater are Category 3 water.

A state environmental document on the Town of Bristol's wastewater system names sump pumps tied into the sewer as a major cause of wet-weather overflows. A basement that fills in a storm should be treated as Category 3 until a crew says otherwise.

More questions about this topic

Is storm flooding in Bristol, RI Category 3 water?

Floodwater from the harbor, river or storm surge is treated as Category 3 because of contamination.
